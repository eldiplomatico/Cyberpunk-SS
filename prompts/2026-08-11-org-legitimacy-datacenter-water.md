# Investigation Tasking — UGA Field Report TP 121 Sponsor Legitimacy & Study Independence

**Date:** 2026-08-11
**Method:** OSINT Entity Profile → Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (Heuer); scoring C = Consistent, I = Inconsistent, A = Ambiguous, NA = Not Applicable
**Trigger:** Instagram community note cites a University of Georgia study on data center water impact as authoritative fact-checking. The study's sponsor, funding source, and independence are unexamined.

---

## Source Material

- **Platform:** Instagram Reels
- **Context:** Video from @celebrityscope (2:22) features an unidentified speaker claiming a data center in Hiawatha has never used a gallon of water. An Instagram community note refutes this claim, citing statistics on evaporative cooling and water consumption industry-wide.
- **Cited authority:** University of Georgia, College of Agricultural & Environmental Sciences, Field Report — *"Understanding How Data Centers Impact Surface and Ground Waters"* (TP 121). Topics: PFAS, Pollution and Contaminants, Water, Well Water. Published on fieldreport.caes.uga.edu. Described as "written and reviewed by experts."
- **Investigation target:** The organization or entity that sponsored, funded, or commissioned TP 121, and whether the study represents independent academic research or interest-driven output.

---

## Analytical Questions

**Group 1 (Sponsor Identification & Legitimacy):** Who funded or commissioned this study, what is the sponsoring entity's organizational profile, and do they have a financial, political, or advocacy stake in the study's conclusions?

**Group 2 (Study Independence & Methodological Integrity):** Does the study's methodology, framing, scope, and conclusion set reflect independent academic inquiry, or does it bear indicators of sponsor influence — selective data, predetermined framing, omitted counterfactuals, or conflicts of interest?

**Group 3 (Instrumentalization & Deployment):** How is this study being deployed in public discourse, and does the pattern of citation — including its appearance as an Instagram community note source — align with a broader campaign by the sponsor or aligned interests?

---

## Hypotheses

### Group 1 — Sponsor Identification & Legitimacy
- **H1: Independent academic research.** TP 121 was produced under standard UGA CAES research funding (federal grants, state agricultural extension budget, university discretionary funds) with no external sponsor dictating scope or conclusions.
- **H2: Industry-funded but editorially independent.** The study was funded by a corporate or trade association sponsor (e.g., water utility, environmental services firm, competing energy sector) under a grant structure that preserved UGA editorial control — sponsor influenced topic selection but not findings.
- **H3: Advocacy-sponsored research.** The study was funded or commissioned by an environmental advocacy organization, anti-data-center group, or NIMBY coalition seeking academic cover for a pre-existing position on data center water consumption.
- **H4: Interested-party capture.** The sponsor has a direct financial interest in the study's conclusions — e.g., a water utility threatened by data center water demand, a competing land-use interest, or a litigation-support entity building an evidentiary record for current or anticipated lawsuits.

### Group 2 — Study Independence & Methodological Integrity
- **H1: Rigorous, balanced methodology.** The study uses standard environmental science methods, addresses counterarguments (closed-loop, air-cooled, immersion), discloses limitations, and its conclusions are proportional to its evidence base.
- **H2: Sound data, skewed framing.** The underlying data is legitimate but the framing, emphasis, or omissions steer the reader toward conclusions favorable to the sponsor — e.g., focusing exclusively on evaporative cooling without acknowledging alternative architectures; emphasizing worst-case water consumption without disclosing the range.
- **H3: Selective scope designed to produce a specific finding.** The study's research questions, site selection, or data boundaries were constrained in ways that guarantee the desired conclusion — e.g., studying only evaporative-cooled facilities, selecting water-stressed geographies, or defining "impact" in ways that exclude mitigation.
- **H4: Legitimate extension research.** The study is a routine CAES extension publication (land-grant university public service mission) addressing a community concern in its service area — no special sponsor, no unusual framing, standard extension-bulletin methodology and caveats.

### Group 3 — Instrumentalization & Deployment
- **H1: Organic citation.** The community note author independently found and cited TP 121 as a credible academic source — no coordination with the sponsor or UGA.
- **H2: Seeded citation.** The sponsor or an aligned entity promoted TP 121 as a go-to reference for community note authors, fact-checkers, or local activists — the study was designed to be cited in exactly these contexts.
- **H3: Litigation or regulatory pipeline.** TP 121 is part of an evidentiary record being assembled for regulatory proceedings, environmental impact challenges, or litigation against data center operators — its public deployment as a fact-check is a secondary use.
- **H4: Extension outreach, organically adopted.** UGA CAES published TP 121 as part of its normal extension mission to inform Georgia communities about local environmental issues; it was adopted into the fact-checking ecosystem without any deliberate seeding by UGA or a sponsor.

## Tasked Searches — 18 Items

### Priority 1 (1–7): Sponsor Identification & Funding Trail
1. **Full text of TP 121.** Retrieve and review the complete study — identify all funding acknowledgments, grant numbers, sponsor disclosures, conflict-of-interest statements, and author affiliations. Note any "supported by" or "prepared for" language.
2. **Author profiles.** Identify all named authors and reviewers. Pull their UGA faculty pages, Google Scholar profiles, ORCID records, research gate activity, and CV/publication lists. Map their funding sources, prior publications, and whether their body of work shows a pattern on the data center water issue or adjacent topics (PFAS, groundwater contamination, industrial water use).
3. **UGA CAES funding disclosures.** Search UGA's grants database, CAES annual reports, and extension program disclosures for any external funding tied to TP 121, its authors, or the Field Report publication series. Check NSF, USDA, EPA, and state-level grant databases.
4. **Field Report publication series.** Characterize the broader Field Report series — is it peer-reviewed, extension-bulletin grade, or something else? How many publications does it carry? What is the editorial review process? Is TP 121 typical of the series or an outlier in topic, length, or framing?
5. **Corporate and NGO donor search.** Check UGA Foundation donor records, CAES advisory board membership, and corporate partnership disclosures for entities with a stake in data center water outcomes — water utilities, environmental law firms, anti-data-center advocacy groups, competing land-use interests (agriculture, real estate).
6. **Georgia-specific data center context.** Are there active or proposed data center developments in Georgia that have triggered community opposition, water-use disputes, or regulatory scrutiny? Map any connection between TP 121's publication timing and local permitting or zoning fights.
7. **PFAS angle.** TP 121 lists PFAS as a topic. Identify whether the PFAS framing connects to a specific regulatory or litigation context — e.g., PFAS contamination lawsuits, EPA regulatory actions, or state-level PFAS legislation in Georgia — that would give a sponsor a motive to link data centers to PFAS contamination.

### Priority 2 (8–13): Methodological Review & Comparative Analysis
8. **Methodology audit.** From the full text: what data sources does TP 121 use? Does it rely on primary field data, literature review, modeling, or a combination? Does it disclose its site selection criteria? Does it address alternative cooling technologies (closed-loop, air-cooled, immersion) or treat evaporative as the only architecture?
9. **Claim verification against independent sources.** The community note states "75–90% of global data centers utilize water cooling systems" — verify this statistic against independent industry sources (Uptime Institute, DOE, IEA, ASHRAE). Is this figure current, outdated, or contested?
10. **Counter-literature search.** Identify studies or industry reports that reach different conclusions on data center water impact — particularly those funded by data center operators or tech trade associations. Map the landscape of competing claims and their respective funders.
11. **Water cycle claim.** The community note states evaporative cooling water "does not reenter the water cycle." Verify this against hydrological science — evaporated water does re-enter the atmospheric water cycle; the claim may conflate local aquifer depletion with global water cycle loss. Determine whether TP 121 makes this claim or whether the community note misrepresents it.
12. **Comparable extension publications.** Identify whether other land-grant universities have published similar data center water impact studies. Compare scope, framing, sponsor disclosures, and conclusions. Is TP 121 an outlier or part of an emerging extension-research pattern?
13. **Peer review status.** Was TP 121 peer-reviewed in the traditional academic sense, or is it an extension bulletin subject only to internal UGA review? The "Written and Reviewed by Experts" branding on Field Report — does "experts" mean external peer reviewers or internal CAES faculty?

### Priority 3 (14–18): Instrumentalization & Deployment Pattern
14. **Community note ecosystem.** How did TP 121 enter the Instagram community notes pipeline? Is it cited in other community notes on data center content? Map all community notes referencing this study across platforms (Instagram, X/Twitter, YouTube).
15. **Citation network.** Beyond community notes — where else is TP 121 cited? Op-eds, regulatory filings, legal briefs, advocacy group materials, local government staff reports, media articles. Map the citation network and identify whether citations cluster around a specific interest group.
16. **Sponsor's public communications.** If a sponsor is identified, review their public statements, press releases, social media, and policy positions on data centers, water use, or PFAS. Does TP 121 serve their stated agenda?
17. **Timing analysis.** When was TP 121 published relative to key events — data center permitting decisions in Georgia, PFAS regulatory milestones, relevant litigation filings, or industry PR campaigns? Temporal proximity to a specific trigger event strengthens instrumentalization hypotheses.
18. **Community note author provenance.** Is there a traceable connection between the community note author who cited TP 121 and the sponsor, UGA CAES, or any anti-data-center advocacy network? Determine whether the fact-check citation was organic or placed.

## Source Tiering Requirements

All findings must carry explicit source tiers:
- **Institutional:** Government filings, academic publications, court records, grant databases, university disclosures.
- **Corporate/Disclosed:** Company filings, foundation records, press releases with named spokespeople.
- **News (corroborated):** Multiple independent outlets with named sources or verifiable documentation.
- **News (single-thread):** Single outlet or single-sourced reporting.
- **Analysis/Opinion:** Trade press analysis, blog posts, expert commentary.
- **SOCMINT (unverified):** Social media content without independent corroboration.

Confidence assessments required for each finding. Flag any finding where the source tier is insufficient to score with confidence.

## Deception/Denial Scan Priorities

1. **Academic laundering.** The highest-priority deception vector: a sponsor with a financial or advocacy stake funds a study through a land-grant university's extension program to acquire the credibility of "peer-reviewed academic research" for what is functionally a position paper. The "Written and Reviewed by Experts" branding on Field Report is the specific mechanism to examine — does the review process substantiate that claim?
2. **PFAS bridging.** PFAS is a high-salience contaminant with active litigation and regulatory momentum. If TP 121 links data centers to PFAS without strong evidentiary basis, the PFAS framing may be a salience hack — borrowing emotional weight from a known-bad contaminant to amplify concern about a separate issue (water consumption volume). Distinguish evidence-based PFAS linkage from rhetorical proximity.
3. **Stat inflation or misattribution.** The "75–90%" figure for evaporative cooling prevalence is the load-bearing statistic in the community note. If it originates from TP 121, verify its source chain. If TP 121 cites a secondary source that itself lacks primary data, the statistic may be circular or outdated.
4. **Organic appearance of coordinated deployment.** If TP 121 was seeded into fact-checking ecosystems by the sponsor or an intermediary, the deployment will be designed to appear organic. Look for coordinated timing across platforms, templated language in multiple community notes, or social media accounts that consistently cite TP 121 in data center discussions.
