## Group 2 Findings — Study Independence & Methodological Integrity

### Finding G2-1: "Temporary Publication" designation means internal review only — not externally peer-reviewed
**Source:** UGA Extension publications/about-publications.html (via web search extract) — institutional tier. High confidence on the review process distinction.
**Group:** 2
**Affects:** H1, H4 (primary); H2, H3 (secondary)
**Scoring:** H1=A H2=C H3=A H4=C
**Matrix change: No — but this is the most important methodological finding.**
**Analytic note:** UGA Extension publication types differ in review rigor. Bulletins (B), Circulars (C), and Agent Resources (AR) require both internal and external peer review before receiving a publication number. Annual Publications (AP) and Temporary Publications (TP) require only internal review. TP publications are designated for "issue- or event-related information that requires immediate dissemination." TP 121 is therefore an internally reviewed extension document, not an externally peer-reviewed academic publication. The Field Report website's "Written and Reviewed by Experts" branding applies to all numbered publications without distinguishing between tiers — technically accurate (internal UGA faculty are experts) but potentially misleading to readers who interpret "expert review" as external peer review. This does not impugn the study's content — many high-quality extension publications undergo internal review only — but it means the study has not been subjected to the level of scrutiny that the branding might imply. This is consistent with both H1 (rigorous methodology can exist without external review) and H4 (standard extension process), while providing fuel for H2 (the framing could overstate the credential).

### Finding G2-2: The "75–90%" evaporative cooling statistic in the community note is unverified and possibly inflated
**Source:** dgtlinfra.com, ScienceDirect, Equinix blog, EESI, MOST Policy Initiative — industry + analysis tier. Moderate confidence.
**Group:** 2
**Affects:** H2, H3 (primary)
**Scoring:** H1=A H2=C H3=C H4=A
**Matrix change: No count change — but fires deception/denial scan priority #3 (stat inflation).**
**Analytic note:** The community note states "About 75-90% of global data centers utilize water cooling systems." No authoritative source for this exact range was found. Equinix (one of the world's largest data center operators) reports that only 40% of its own facilities use evaporative cooling globally. The Uptime Institute and DOE do not appear to publish a single definitive percentage. The statistic may conflate "water cooling" (which includes closed-loop systems that consume minimal water) with "evaporative cooling" (which consumes water through evaporation). This is a critical distinction — the community note's argument depends on the claim that most data centers are evaporative, but the evidence base for this claim is thin. **It is currently unknown whether this statistic originates from TP 121 or was introduced by the community note author independently.** Full-text review of TP 121 is required to determine attribution.

### Finding G2-3: The PFAS–data center connection is substantiated but operates on a different axis than water consumption volume
**Source:** Bay Journal, EESI, CHIPS Communities United, Moms Clean Air Force, Earthjustice — advocacy + news tier. Moderate-high confidence on the PFAS-in-data-centers claim; lower confidence on the scale of contamination.
**Group:** 2
**Affects:** H1, H2 (primary)
**Scoring:** H1=A H2=A H3=A H4=A
**Matrix change: No — scored ambiguous across all hypotheses because the relationship between TP 121's PFAS content and its water-quantity claims cannot be assessed without the full text.**
**Analytic note:** The PFAS–data center link is real: PFAS-based coolants are used in two-phase immersion cooling (Chemours is a major supplier); fire suppression systems in data centers use PFAS-containing agents (FM-200, Novec 1230); and Chemours operations have contaminated 7,000+ drinking water wells in North Carolina. However, PFAS contamination and water consumption volume are distinct environmental concerns. TP 121 lists both "PFAS" and "Water" as topics. If the study treats them as separate impact vectors with distinct evidence chains, this is sound environmental science. If the study or its citation conflates them — using PFAS's emotional salience to amplify concern about water quantity — this is the salience hack identified in deception/denial scan priority #2. The community note does not mention PFAS, which may indicate the community note author selected only the water-quantity findings. Cannot score without full text.

### Finding G2-4: The water-cycle claim in the community note is hydrologically imprecise
**Source:** Hydrological science fundamentals — established science. High confidence.
**Group:** 2
**Affects:** H2, H3 (primary)
**Scoring:** H1=NA H2=C H3=C H4=NA
**Matrix change: No count change — but this is a framing flag.**
**Analytic note:** The community note states that "the water used by the datacenter does not reenter into the water cycle." This is incorrect as stated. Evaporated water re-enters the atmospheric water cycle (evaporation → condensation → precipitation). The accurate claim is that evaporated water does not return to the *local* aquifer or watershed from which it was drawn, creating a local water deficit even though the water is not destroyed globally. Industry sources cite ~80% evaporation rates for data center water, with ~20% discharged to wastewater treatment. The imprecision matters: if TP 121 makes the correct local-deficit argument, the community note author degraded it into a scientifically inaccurate absolute claim. If TP 121 contains the same imprecision, this is a methodological weakness. Again — full text required.

## Cross-Group Bridge Analysis — Annex B

### Bridge 1→2 (Sponsor → Methodology): If no sponsor, does framing bias still matter?
**Mechanism:** Even without an external sponsor, the TP fast-track (internal review only) could allow framing choices that external review would catch — selective emphasis, imprecise claims, omitted counterfactuals. An institutional incentive (UGA CAES responding to public concern in its service area) can produce the same framing skew as a sponsor, via institutional rather than financial motivation.
**Evidence chain:** G1-3 (Georgia crisis context) + G2-1 (TP internal review) + G2-2 (unverified statistic) + G2-4 (imprecise water-cycle claim).
**Confidence:** MODERATE. The bridge mechanism is plausible but unconfirmed — the framing distortions observed so far may be the community note author's, not TP 121's.

### Bridge 2→3 (Methodology → Deployment): Does the community note degrade the study?
**Mechanism:** The community note may introduce distortions absent from TP 121 itself — inflating the "75–90%" figure, making the water-cycle claim absolute rather than local, and omitting the PFAS-vs-volume distinction. If so, the study is being instrumentalized not by its sponsor (none found) but by its downstream citators. The "Written and Reviewed by Experts" branding lends borrowed authority that the community note exploits.
**Evidence chain:** G2-2 (unverified statistic) + G2-4 (imprecise claim) + G3-1 (no seeding found) + G3-2 (timing).
**Confidence:** MODERATE-HIGH. This is the most likely distortion pathway: a legitimate study degraded in citation.

### Integrated Assessment
The least-disconfirmed explanation across all three groups is that TP 121 is a legitimate UGA Cooperative Extension product — responsive to a real community crisis in its service area, authored by a qualified career scientist, produced under standard institutional processes — whose deployment in the Instagram community note ecosystem introduced framing distortions that the study's "Written and Reviewed by Experts" branding inadvertently shields from scrutiny. The organization behind the study is the University of Georgia. The "sponsor" is the Georgia taxpayer and the USDA cooperative extension system. The risk is not captured research but degraded citation.

## Deception/Denial Outcomes

1. **Academic laundering — NOT FIRED.** No external sponsor was found. The TP designation and internal-only review are standard UGA extension processes, not a mechanism for laundering an advocacy position through academic credibility. The "Written and Reviewed by Experts" branding is a general Field Report feature, not specific to TP 121. Residual risk: if an external sponsor exists but is not publicly disclosed, this assessment would change.
2. **PFAS bridging — INDETERMINATE.** Cannot assess without TP 121 full text. The community note does not mention PFAS, which may indicate the community note author did not exploit this vector. But TP 121 lists PFAS as a topic — the study may legitimately address PFAS as a separate contamination pathway, or it may conflate the two concerns. Collection priority for next cycle.
3. **Stat inflation — PARTIALLY FIRED.** The "75–90%" evaporative cooling prevalence claim has no verified authoritative source. Equinix's own data (40% of its DCs globally) contradicts the high end. The statistic may conflate "water cooling" (including closed-loop) with "evaporative cooling" (open-loop, water-consuming). Attribution to TP 121 vs. the community note author is unknown. This is the load-bearing claim and the highest-priority item for full-text verification.
4. **Organic appearance of coordinated deployment — NOT FIRED.** No coordination evidence found. The citation appears organic. Residual risk: seeding is designed to be invisible, and absence of evidence is not conclusive.
